Personal project · 2024 – Present

Off-Grid Solar & IoT Energy Monitoring System

What started as a £40 Facebook Marketplace kit to stop fumbling around a dark garden shed grew into a 3.2kW roof-mounted hybrid solar system powering the house — with a custom ESP32/ESPHome monitoring stack built to fix an inverter that couldn't even report its own battery level correctly.

01 Origins — lighting a shed (2024)

The whole thing started small: it was hard to find anything in the garden shed after dark. In 2024 I picked up a cheap 200W solar kit off Facebook Marketplace for £40 — two 100W panels, a charge controller and some wiring — and paired it with a couple of used 12V car batteries from a friend. Enough to keep a light on at night, and enough to get me hooked.

Early shed setup — the original 200W kit and car batteries

02 Going bigger

Lighting a shed led to reading about how countries like Afghanistan and Pakistan use solar to work around unreliable grids — load shedding, and never quite knowing when the power would come back. That reframed the shed project for me: this wasn't just a hobby setup, it was a genuinely useful way to take load off an unreliable supply. The goal became mainly about saving money on grid imports and being less dependent on the grid overall. In May 2025 I started applying that thinking to the house.

03 The home system (May 2025)

The core of the system is a PowMR 6.2kW 48V hybrid inverter, which takes the solar input, converts it to the right voltage to charge the battery bank, and produces 240V AC to run regular household electrics.

For batteries, I went with 2× 24V 100Ah LiFePO4 packs wired in series to make a 48V bank (5.2kWh). Solar generation comes from 6× Longi Hi-Mo X6 HTH 535W panels — 3.2kW at peak. Budget meant the panels couldn't go on the roof straight away, so initially 4 of the 6 went up on the garden fence, which was enough to charge the batteries through the afternoon. Even in that early state it was already useful: running the washing machine and dryer off the battery bank saved roughly 2kWh per load.

04 The monitoring problem

Once the system was running, I noticed the inverter's own battery percentage reading wasn't accurate — not close enough to trust. Rather than live with a rough estimate, I looked into battery shunts, which measure the actual current flowing in and out of the battery bank directly, giving a proper state-of-charge figure instead of the inverter's guess.

05 The build

Getting the data into Home Assistant meant tackling two separate problems:

  • Reading the inverter: I got an ESP32 talking to the inverter over its RS232 port, using a cut-up Ethernet cable to make the physical connection — a scrappy but effective way to wire it up without a proper adapter.
  • Reading the shunt: a separate D1 mini (ESP8266) board, flashed with ESPHome, reads the shunt and sends battery data to Home Assistant over WiFi.

With both feeding into Home Assistant, I built a custom Lovelace dashboard card configured to present the data clearly — generation, load, state of charge, charge/discharge power — so it's easy for non-technical people in the house to understand at a glance, not just me.

06 Roof mount (April–May 2026)

In April 2026 I finally had the budget for proper roof mounts, and by mid-May the full array was installed on the garage roof. That moved generation from a few afternoon hours on the fence to spread across the whole day — a big step up in how much of the system's potential was actually being used.

Photo placeholder

Panels roof-mounted on the garage

07 Results

With the array roof-mounted and generating all day, July 2026 gives a clear before/after: the house imported 141kWh from the grid overnight, while the panels produced around 340kWh during the day and evening — saving roughly £85 that month at 25p/kWh. The system is still growing, and the monitoring stack means every improvement from here is measurable rather than guesswork.
